Our faith, and our life together at MNSU

Gibi Gubae exists so that Ethiopian Orthodox students at Mankato have a home away from home — and so that anyone curious about one of Christianity's oldest living traditions has a welcoming place to ask questions.

Christianity comes to Aksum

Ethiopia's Christian history begins in the 4th century, in the ancient kingdom of Aksum. Tradition holds that a shipwrecked traveler named Frumentius, along with his brother Aedesius, won the trust of the royal court and eventually tutored the young prince Ezana. When Ezana became king, he was baptized by Frumentius and made Christianity the religion of the Aksumite state, around the year 330. Frumentius later traveled to Alexandria, where he was consecrated Ethiopia's first bishop by St. Athanasius, and returned home to continue the work of the church he had started.

The Nine Saints and the birth of Ge'ez Christianity

About a century and a half later, a group of monks remembered as the Nine Saints arrived in Aksum from the Eastern Roman Empire, likely by way of a monastery in Egypt. They learned Ge'ez, the language of the kingdom, and set out across the countryside founding monasteries, overturning old centers of pagan worship, and translating scripture and religious writing into Ge'ez for the first time. Their students carried the work further still — one of them, St. Yared, is credited with composing the tones and hymns still sung in Ethiopian Orthodox worship today. The monasteries the Nine Saints founded, like Debre Damo, remain centers of pilgrimage and learning to this day.

The Kebra Nagast and the Solomonic line

Ethiopian Orthodox identity is bound up with the Kebra Nagast, "The Glory of the Kings," a Ge'ez epic that tells of the Queen of Sheba's journey to King Solomon in Jerusalem, and of their son, Menelik I, who is said to have brought the Ark of the Covenant back to Ethiopia. For centuries this story underwrote the legitimacy of Ethiopia's Solomonic emperors, and it still shapes how the church understands its own place in salvation history — as a people who received the Ark, and with it a direct thread back to ancient Israel.

An independent church

For most of its history, the Ethiopian church's senior bishop, or Abuna, was appointed from the Coptic Orthodox Church of Alexandria — a relationship going back to Frumentius himself. That changed in the 20th century: in 1951 an Ethiopian-born bishop, Abuna Basilios, was consecrated Archbishop of Ethiopia, and in 1959 the Coptic Pope enthroned him as the first Patriarch of an independent, self-governing Ethiopian Orthodox Church. Today the church is led by its own Patriarch and Holy Synod in Addis Ababa, while remaining in communion with the other Oriental Orthodox churches — the Coptic, Eritrean, Armenian, Syriac, and Malankara traditions.

Worship in Ge'ez

Services are chanted in Ge'ez, accompanied by the sistrum, drum, and prayer staff, and structured around the Divine Liturgy. Many of our gatherings blend Ge'ez, Amharic, and English, so that students who didn't grow up with the language can still follow and participate.

A rhythm of fasting and feast

Fasting is one of the most distinctive parts of Ethiopian Orthodox life. The church calendar moves through extended fasting periods — including a long fast before Easter — alternating with major feasts that bring the community together to celebrate. It's a rhythm that shapes how we eat, gather, and mark the year, not just something practiced alone.

Why we started this community

College can pull students away from the church community they grew up with. Gibi Gubae was formed so that doesn't have to happen at MNSU — a place to keep praying, fasting, and studying scripture together, and a door open to any student, Orthodox or not, who wants to understand the faith better.

New here?

You're welcome at either gathering, no background required. Come, sit, listen, and ask as many questions as you want.
